Symptom Survey — the structured wide net

When a patient's illness may not have a name yet, the Symptom Survey (/symptom-survey) is how you cast the wide net: a coded review-of-systems where every answer is Present, Absent, or Unknown. Recording "asked and absent" is what lets an outbreak's case definition tighten later — you can only drop a symptom that turns out to be common if the survey actually asked about it.

Why three states, not free text

A symptom you dismiss in a free-text note is invisible, not absent — and invisible can't be analyzed. The survey makes the distinction explicit: Unknown means "not asked" and is never treated as absent. Leave an item Unknown only when it genuinely wasn't assessed.

Using it

  1. Enter the patient and Load. The question set is the core screen plus any symptoms the currently active clusters are watching, grouped by body system.
  2. Answer Present / Absent for what you assessed; leave the rest Unknown.
  3. Save answers. Only non-Unknown answers are recorded.
  4. Screen against active clusters to see whether this patient matches an emerging cluster, with a score. If they do, Suggest into cluster flags them for the epidemiologist to review — a human suggestion persists even if a later evaluation stops matching.

When nothing matches

If the screen comes back with no match, you get a second option: "Start a cluster from this patient". This is the gesture for this doesn't look like anything I know — the case that previously had nowhere to go, because suggesting a patient into a cluster required a cluster to already exist.

It drafts a cluster from what you have recorded:

  • the symptoms you marked Present;
  • any etiologic test that came back negative — the workup that found nothing is as much a part of the picture as the positives;
  • any result the laboratory flagged abnormal.

The patient is added as the index case, and the draft goes to the epidemiology team.

A draft reaches no chart. It does not appear on this patient's cover sheet or anyone else's, produces no precaution banner, and is not screened against other patients, until an epidemiologist reviews and activates it. That boundary is deliberate: naming a cluster tends to create its population — once a named condition is visible, ambiguous cases get attributed to it, which grows the group, which makes it look confirmed. Assembling the draft is automated; publishing it is not.
A definition built from one patient fits that patient perfectly and generalises to nothing. The draft says so on its face, and the epidemiologist is expected to broaden the features and weights before activating it. You are giving them a starting point, not a case definition.
Recording symptoms needs a front-door key (PROVIDER, ORES, ORELSE, or EPI MANAGER) — nurses can do it. Proposing a draft cluster needs the same key, so you do not need EPI MANAGER to raise the flag. Once an epidemiologist confirms the patient into a cluster, an isolation precaution banner appears on the patient's cover sheet.
